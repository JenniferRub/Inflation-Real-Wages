Inflation and Real Wage Growth in the United States¶

Question: How has inflation affected real wage growth in the United States over the last 20 years?

Expectation (from the plan): Periods of higher inflation should go with lower real wage growth. When prices rise faster than wages, workers lose purchasing power. The expectation is supported if the regression of real wage growth on inflation has a negative slope. It is contradicted if the slope is positive or close to zero.

Real wage growth is roughly nominal wage growth minus inflation, so part of that negative slope is built in by definition. Step 7 therefore also regresses nominal wage growth on inflation. A slope below 1 there means wages do not keep up with prices, which is the real test of the expectation.

Step 3: Clean the data and build the growth rates¶

  1. Real wage: Divide the nominal hourly wage by CPI. Rescale so the result is in dollars at the most recent month's prices.
  2. Year-over-year growth: Take the percent change from the same month one year earlier. Do this for CPI (inflation), nominal wages, and real wages. Comparing to the same month a year ago keeps seasonal patterns from distorting the result.
  3. Last 20 years: Keep only the most recent 20 years. The earnings series starts in March 2006, and each growth rate needs 12 earlier months. So the sample actually starts in early 2007.
In [4]:
YEARS_TO_KEEP = 20


def yoy_percent_change(series):
    """Percent change from the same month one year earlier."""
    return series.pct_change(12) * 100


def build_dataset(raw, years):
    """Add real wages and year-over-year growth rates, then keep the last `years` years."""
    data = raw.copy()
    data["real_wage"] = data["hourly_wage"] * data["cpi"].iloc[-1] / data["cpi"]
    data["inflation"] = yoy_percent_change(data["cpi"])
    data["nominal_wage_growth"] = yoy_percent_change(data["hourly_wage"])
    data["real_wage_growth"] = yoy_percent_change(data["real_wage"])
    data = data.dropna()
    start_date = data.index[-1] - pd.DateOffset(years=years)
    return data[data.index >= start_date]


wage_data = build_dataset(raw_data, YEARS_TO_KEEP)
print(f"Sample: {wage_data.index[0]:%b %Y} to {wage_data.index[-1]:%b %Y} ({len(wage_data)} months)")
wage_data[["inflation", "nominal_wage_growth", "real_wage_growth"]].describe().round(2)

Step 5: Regression of real wage growth on inflation¶

The model is a simple linear regression on monthly data:

$$\text{real wage growth} = a + b \times \text{inflation}$$

A negative slope $b$ supports the expectation. fit_line uses np.polyfit to estimate the intercept and slope, and reports R² as well. regression_table runs the fit twice, so the same check can be repeated for nominal wages in Step 7.

The second row is a robustness check that drops 2020–2021. In 2020 many low-wage workers lost their jobs, so average hourly earnings went up even though no individual got a raise. That spike could distort the fit.

In [6]:
def fit_line(x, y):
    """Fit y = intercept + slope * x by least squares and return the results."""
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
    r_squared = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1] ** 2
    return {"slope": slope, "intercept": intercept, "r_squared": r_squared, "months": len(x)}


is_pandemic_period = (wage_data.index >= "2020-01-01") & (wage_data.index < "2022-01-01")
excl_pandemic = wage_data[~is_pandemic_period]


def regression_table(y_column):
    """Regress `y_column` on inflation for the full sample and excluding 2020-2021."""
    table = pd.DataFrame({
        "Full sample": fit_line(wage_data["inflation"], wage_data[y_column]),
        "Excluding 2020-2021": fit_line(excl_pandemic["inflation"], excl_pandemic[y_column]),
    }).T
    return table.astype({"months": int})


real_wage_results = regression_table("real_wage_growth")
real_wage_results.round(3)

def plot_fit(y_column, fit, title, ylabel):
    """Scatter `y_column` against inflation and draw the fitted regression line."""
    inflation_range = np.linspace(wage_data["inflation"].min(), wage_data["inflation"].max(), 100)
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))
    ax.scatter(wage_data["inflation"], wage_data[y_column], alpha=0.6, label="One month")
    ax.plot(
        inflation_range,
        fit["intercept"] + fit["slope"] * inflation_range,
        color="tab:orange",
        linewidth=2,
        label=f"Fitted line: slope = {fit['slope']:.2f}, R² = {fit['r_squared']:.2f}",
    )
    add_chart_labels(
        ax,
        title=title,
        xlabel="Inflation, change in CPI from a year earlier (%)",
        ylabel=ylabel,
    )
    ax.legend(frameon=False)
    fig.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

Step 7: Is the negative slope built in? Nominal wage growth on inflation¶

Real wage growth is roughly nominal wage growth minus inflation. If nominal wages did not react to inflation at all, the Step 5 slope would be about −1 just from that definition. So the negative slope alone does not show much.

The better test regresses nominal wage growth on inflation:

$$\text{nominal wage growth} = a + c \times \text{inflation}$$

  • $c = 1$: wages rise one-for-one with prices, and real wages are unaffected by inflation.
  • $0 < c < 1$: wages rise with inflation, but not by enough, so real wages fall. This supports the expectation.
  • $c \le 0$: wages do not respond to inflation at all.

The two regressions are linked: the real-wage slope is approximately $c - 1$. The last line of the output checks this.

Conclusion¶

The expectation is supported, but the evidence comes from the nominal wage regression. In the data available when this was written (through August 2026), each extra percentage point of inflation matched about 0.7 percentage points less real wage growth (R² ≈ 0.64). Most of that slope is built in, because real wage growth is roughly nominal wage growth minus inflation.

The nominal regression in Step 7 is the real test. Each extra point of inflation went with only about 0.3 points of extra nominal wage growth (R² ≈ 0.25), and about 0.35 when 2020–2021 are dropped (R² ≈ 0.44). Wages do rise with prices, but only by about a third as much, so real wages fall when inflation rises. This shows up in 2008, 2011, and 2021–2023. 2022 is the clearest case: nominal wages rose about 5.4%, but prices rose about 8%.

Limitations

  • Correlation, not causation: The models have no controls, such as unemployment, productivity, or energy prices, that could drive both variables.
  • Overlapping data: Year-over-year rates from consecutive months share 11 months of data, so the monthly observations are not independent. That makes the fits look more precise than they are.
  • Wages adjust with a delay: Wage contracts are often set once a year, so wages may catch up with inflation later. These regressions only compare the same month and miss any delayed catch-up.
  • Composition effects: Average hourly earnings change when the mix of workers changes, as it did in 2020. The series also covers only private-sector employees.
  • Short history: The earnings series starts in 2006, so the 20 years include just a few inflation episodes.
